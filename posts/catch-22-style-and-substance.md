A potential temptation when analyzing writing is to treat style and substance separately. That is, we assume that a work’s execution and the opinions it seeks to convey can be evaluated independently. Under this lens, a piece of writing may be deemed outstanding from a technical viewpoint simply because its structure or style is elegant. But this boundary is superficial and arbitrary. In truly good writing, the style utilized is deeply tied to the substance conveyed because structure itself is able to deliver meaning, and it should therefore be shaped to reflect the thoughts that the writing intends to communicate. If a text lacks a deep, non-trivial opinion, its structure loses its purpose and those choices become meaningless and arbitrary decorations. Catch-22 can perhaps serve as the ultimate proof (or contradiction) of this dynamic. On the surface, the Catch-22 employs a deceptively simple, circular narrative that many people often reduce to the superficial anti-war argument that “war is an unpredictable hell.” But Catch-22’s technical execution is not a series of arbitrary cyclical story lines. It is precisely engineered to describe a terrifying philosophy: that modern bureaucracy is a self-preserving, fake reality that distorts logic to ensure its immortality.

If Joseph Heller wrote 500 pages of Catch-22 simply to say that war is unpredictable and arbitrary, the book would insist upon itself. The fractured timeline and repetitiveness just to convey such a basic opinion would make Catch-22 a massive failure. Catch-22 is beautiful because its structure is anchored to an opinion that is far more profound: Catch-22 argues that the leviathan that is modern state infrastructure (which, in the book, is represented by the military-industrial complex) has evolved like cancer and is completely out of human control.

The way Catch-22 handles time is the most obvious proof that technique and substance are inherently linked. Catch-22 famously handles time non-linearly, and shifts between the past and present timelines abruptly. A purely mechanical reading of this structural choice would indicate that Heller was trying to mimic the confusion, chaos, and disorder associated with war. This would be a weak interpretation. Time is one of the few features that can normally be grounded in reality, as it is unidirectional and forces an increase in entropy. The scrambling of time in Catch-22 removes this naturalness, because in the eyes of the bureaucracy, progress is not measured by how close the Allied Powers are to victory nor is it measured by the number of days, weeks, and months that have passed. Rather, it is measured by the arbitrary raising of required combat missions. Bureaucracy can amend history, falsify records, and declare that living soldiers are dead (as is the case with Doc Daneeka). The only true objective reality in Catch-22 is what gets committed to and written in paperwork. From that lens, the non-linear structure of Catch-22 is really the only structurally reasonable way to map the world it has constructed. The hard evidence for this view lies in Catch-22’s only linear element: the required number of combat missions that airmen must fly. While the physical timeline is fractured, a close reading of Catch-22 will reveal that this quota monotonically increases, serving as the book’s replacement for time, the most foundational indicator of reality.

In Catch-22, Yossarian tries to get grounded from flight duty, and is promptly told by Doc Daneeka that to stop flying, it must be determined that he is crazy and therefore unfit to fly. But in asking to stop flying, he would demonstrate a concern for his safety, thereby implying sanity. Thus, anyone who asks to stop flying is sane, and must keep flying. On a purely structural level, this logic seems to simply mirror the absurdity of war. But a deeper reading reveals that it matches Catch-22’s stance that power structures distort logic to feed themselves by creating a structure where an exit condition is structurally impossible. Without such a profound understanding of how language can be weaponized to supersede logic and trap the human mind, Heller could not have constructed the “catch” in such a beautifully simple way, yet have it be so psychologically inescapable.

Ultimately, Catch-22 stands as proof that it is impossible to write a technically great book, with a trivial intellectual foundation. Without the deeper philosophy that Catch-22 expresses, its writing style and structure are hollow and insane. Technical excellence cannot exist in a vacuum, and Catch-22 clearly shows that a piece of writing can only be structurally sound if the writer actually has something meaningful to say .
